About the project

Two students, one question: what else does the treatment hit?

CyanoSelect (first released as the Selectivity Tool) was built by Arjun Pendharkar and Siddharth Santhosh, seniors at Green Level High School in Cary, North Carolina, under the supervision of Dr. Bharathi Pandi of NC STEM Academy. CyanoSelect has two parts: a ranking of treatments from published lab data, and a pond simulator.

Why we did it

It started with a news story.

In June 2022, ABC11 reported that dogs became ill after swimming at Jordan Lake, and at least one died, after toxic algae turned up in the water.

That one hit close to home. Arjun's dog, Oreo, goes on regular walks near Jordan Lake, and it was easy to picture Oreo being one of those dogs. We wanted to know how a bloom like that gets treated, and whether the treatment itself could hurt the rest of the lake.

Each registered algaecide comes with a label, but we could not find a side-by-side comparison of how the options affect the organisms a lake manager wants to keep. So we built one. The question turned out to be the perfect mix of biology and technology, which let us combine both of our specialties.

Who it is for

Anyone who has to choose a treatment and then live with the lake afterward.

The output is a ranked table with the reasoning attached, so it can be read, argued with, and reproduced.

Project timeline

How we got here.

  1. June 2022 Dogs fall ill after swimming at Jordan Lake

    The news story that started the question.

  2. September 2026 Expert review and the diquat correction

    We had paired mismatched studies. Taking both numbers from the same study moved diquat from last to first among single treatments, and "match the tests" became the tool's central rule.

  3. September 30, 2026 Field visit to the Cary/Apex Water Treatment Facility

    The plant handles blooms on site rather than in the lake, so we now build for the people managing ponds and smaller lakes. See the photos.

  4. October 15, 2026 Talk at the North Carolina Lake Management Society Fall Workshop

    We presented the tool and its rankings to lake and pond managers in Greensboro, and took advice from professionals in the field. See the photos.

  5. November 2026 Pond simulator added

    A simulator that follows one treatment through a pond for 14 days, checked by 284 automated tests. Its outputs are model predictions. Try it.

  6. Next Laboratory validation

    Matched tests of copper, hydrogen peroxide and diquat on green algae and water fleas, using more than one Microcystis strain.

Outreach

Inside the Plant

Our visit to the Cary/Apex Water Treatment Facility

On September 30, 2026, we toured the Cary/Apex Water Treatment Facility, which treats Jordan Lake water for more than 310,000 people in Cary, Apex, Morrisville, and part of Research Triangle Park. The visit changed how we frame our project. Jordan Lake is operated by the US Army Corps of Engineers, and algae is not treated in the lake itself. Instead, the plant manages it on site, adjusting activated carbon to measured taste-and-odor compounds and using ozone and biofiltration. That is why our tool is aimed at ponds and smaller lakes, where in-lake treatment actually happens.

Thank you to the facility's laboratory team for hosting us. Facility figures are from the Town of Cary's virtual tour.

What we learned from people in the field

The workshop let us learn directly from the professionals who manage North Carolina's lakes and ponds.

  • Advice from the North Carolina Department of Environmental Quality. We talked with Mark Vanderborgh of the North Carolina Department of Environmental Quality and took his advice on the project and on how a tool like ours fits the way blooms are handled in the state.
  • Conversations with practitioners. Between sessions we talked with lake managers, applicators and other people who work in the field about how treatment decisions actually get made, and what they would need from a tool like ours.
  • Presenting our solution. Explaining the tool to the North Carolina Lake Management Society, an audience that treats lakes and ponds for a living, pushed us to make the method clear and to be upfront about what published data can and cannot tell a lake manager.

What we heard is shaping the next version of the tool, alongside the laboratory validation we have planned.

What's next

The predictions are the easy part. Testing them is the work.

The framework is complete. Everything below is what turns it from a set of predictions into validated findings.

1

Validation

Test the rankings in a lab

Planned

Three treatments that can be sourced as pure compounds and dosed precisely: copper sulfate, hydrogen peroxide, and diquat dibromide. Three organisms: Microcystis aeruginosa as the target, Raphidocelis subcapitata as the green alga, and Daphnia magna as the grazer.

3treatments
3organisms
~360test units

Each treatment and each predicted pair will be tested across a five-point dose series plus a control, following the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development test guidelines 201 for the algae and 202 for Daphnia. That comes to roughly 360 individual test units.

The rule that makes it worth doing: every organism is measured at the same exposure time using the same endpoint. Much of the published literature does not do this, and mismatched exposure times are one of the reasons reported selectivity can be inflated. Generating matched data is the point.

Question 01

Does the measured ranking match the predicted ranking?

Question 02

Does each combination beat, match, or fall short of its Bliss prediction? Beating it means real synergy. Matching it means the framework is calibrated. Falling short means antagonism.

All three outcomes are reportable. The experiment is designed so that it cannot be failed, only answered.

2

Data

Fill in the missing data

Only 15 of 52 target-kill cells are currently filled. Some of those gaps are genuine absences in the literature, and some are values locked behind paywalls or buried in regulatory documents. Every cell filled makes the ranking more stable. Priorities are additional target-kill values for the three under-covered cyanobacteria genera, and cross-validating the values that currently rest on a single source.

3

Outreach

Put it in front of practitioners

A tool that only makes sense to its authors is not useful. We have begun reaching out to watershed and riverkeeper organizations, state water quality agencies, and university researchers working on cyanobacterial blooms, to find out whether the framework reflects how treatment decisions actually get made in the field.

The most useful response so far has been expert review of the data and wording, which led to the September 2026 diquat correction and to several of the limitations listed above.

4

Scope

Extend what it can score

Longer term, three directions. Extend the ranking to the other three target genera once the data supports it. Add treatments beyond the current thirteen, including compounds that are promising but not commercially available. And test whether the Bliss assumption holds across mechanisms, since agents that share a mode of action are the most likely to break it.
