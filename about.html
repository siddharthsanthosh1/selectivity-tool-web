Why we did it

It started with a news story.

In June 2022, ABC11 reported that dogs became ill after swimming at Jordan Lake, and at least one died, after toxic algae turned up in the water.

That one hit close to home. Arjun's dog, Oreo, goes on regular walks near Jordan Lake, and it was easy to picture Oreo being one of those dogs. We wanted to know how a bloom like that gets treated, and whether the treatment itself could hurt the rest of the lake.

Each registered algaecide comes with a label, but we could not find a side-by-side comparison of how the options affect the organisms a lake manager wants to keep. So we built one. The question turned out to be the perfect mix of biology and technology, which let us combine both of our specialties.

Project timeline

How we got here.

  1. June 2022 Dogs fall ill after swimming at Jordan Lake

    The news story that started the question.

  2. September 2026 Expert review and the diquat correction

    We had paired mismatched studies. Taking both numbers from the same study moved diquat from last to first among single treatments, and "match the tests" became the tool's central rule.

  3. September 30, 2026 Field visit to the Cary/Apex Water Treatment Facility

    The plant handles blooms on site rather than in the lake, so we now build for the people managing ponds and smaller lakes. See the photos.

  4. October 15, 2026 Talk at the NC Lake Management Society Fall Workshop

    We presented the tool and its rankings to lake and pond managers in Greensboro, and took advice from professionals in the field. See the photos.

  5. Next Laboratory validation

    Matched tests of copper, hydrogen peroxide and diquat on green algae and water fleas, using more than one Microcystis strain.
