Rank treatments

Which treatment spares the rest of the lake?

Every treatment and predicted pair, scored against Microcystis aeruginosa on its predicted risk to green algae, water fleas and fish.

  1. Each score is a selectivity index (SI): the dose that harms a bystander divided by the dose that controls the bloom. Above 1, the bloom is hit first.
  2. Treatments are ranked by their weakest bystander score (min-SI), never an average.
  3. Pairs are predictions from Bliss independence, not lab results. The math, step by step.

Try it yourself

Run the ranking in your browser.

The same math as the archived Python tool, running locally on this page, with the corrected diquat values. A selectivity index (SI) is a bystander EC50 divided by the bloom EC50; an EC50 is the dose that causes a 50% effect in a lab test. Change the metric, drop an axis, remove a treatment, and watch the ranking reorder.

Ranked by the weakest non-target axis. A treatment is only as selective as its worst link.

Under Bliss independence, each of a combination's scores falls between its two ingredients' scores. When two treatments are weak on different organisms, the combination's weakest score can end up higher than either one alone. With the matched diquat values, copper plus diquat does exactly that. Lab testing would show whether the prediction holds, or whether the pair interacts.

These are predictions from published data, not measurements. The Python implementation archived at the DOI is the citable reference for the method. The diquat values on this page were corrected in September 2026 and differ from the archived release.

LimitationsWhat this tool does not tell you.

Read this before using the output. Every item here is a constraint we can state precisely, which is the point: each one is testable, and the planned lab work is designed to test them.

Predictions are not measurements.

Every combination value in the ranking is a hypothesis generated under stated assumptions. None of the combinations has been measured in a lab. Validation is planned, and both agreement and disagreement with the predictions are reportable results.

The input data is sparse.

The published literature contains comparable target-kill values for only a fraction of treatment and organism pairs. The rankings rest on relatively few fully scored treatments, so a single new measurement can move them.

Exposure durations do not always match.

Some published values compare target and non-target toxicity measured over different exposure times and endpoints. Because the target value sits in the denominator of the index, a mismatch inflates apparent selectivity. Affected entries are flagged in the code.

Bliss independence can be violated.

The combination model assumes the two agents act independently. Agents that share a mechanism (copper and hydrogen peroxide both act partly through oxidative stress) may not. Where the lab disagrees with the prediction, that disagreement is the finding.

It ranks by selectivity, not efficacy.

A treatment can kill the target extremely well and still rank poorly here. That is intended behavior, not a bug. This tool answers one question (what is the predicted risk to non-target organisms) and should sit alongside efficacy, cost, and permitting, not replace them.

Water chemistry changes toxicity.

Toxicity varies with water chemistry. Copper in particular is far more toxic in soft, low-alkalinity water than in hard water.

Strains differ.

Microcystis strains respond differently to the same product.

Microcystis is not the only bloom organism.

Other bloom organisms occur in North Carolina (for example Euglena and Prymnesium), and some treatments target green algae blooms instead.

Copper products are not all alike.

Chelated copper products are generally lower risk than copper sulfate. Copper is also toxic to mollusks, which this tool does not score.

Lab water is not lake water.

Lab values measured in clean water can differ substantially from values in natural water.
