Selectivity Tool · The problem

Control is one question. Risk is the other.

Toxic cyanobacteria blooms can close beaches, threaten drinking water, and harm pets and wildlife. Several treatments can control a bloom, and choosing between them is a real decision.

Jordan Lake, North Carolina, June 2022

Dogs became ill after swimming at Jordan Lake, and at least one died. Chatham County issued a public warning about toxic algae in the water, and told people to keep themselves and their pets out of it.

ABC11: dog death linked to water toxins at Jordan Lake

Treatments are usually compared on how well they control the bloom. This tool adds a comparison of predicted risk to green algae, zooplankton, and fish, to help identify the lowest-risk option.

Each of the three groups is scored because it plays a distinct role in the lake:

Green algae

Primary producers at the base of the food web, and the standard non-target algae in toxicity testing.

Water fleas

Zooplankton that graze algae and feed fish, which makes them part of the lake’s natural check on algae.

Fish

The visible top of the system, and the reason most people care about the lake in the first place.

Most comparisons ask which treatment controls the bloom. This tool also asks which one does it with the lowest predicted risk to everything else.

Who it is for

Anyone who has to choose a treatment and then live with the lake afterward.

The output is a ranked table with the reasoning attached, so it can be read, argued with, and reproduced.

Lake managers

Comparing treatment options for a specific waterbody.

Water utilities

Protecting source water while weighing non-target risk.

Watershed nonprofits

Asking informed questions about a proposed treatment plan.

Researchers

Generating testable hypotheses about treatment combinations.

Regulators

Weighing permit decisions against non-target impact.

Framework complete. Laboratory validation planned.

Model predictions, not a treatment recommendation. CyanoSelect ranks treatments from published lab data and simulates ponds; none of its predictions has been tested in a real pond yet. Registered products have undergone risk assessment by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, and the product label governs how they may be used. Follow the label and your state’s rules, and consult a licensed aquatic applicator or your state agency before treating any water body.

Built by Arjun Pendharkar and Siddharth Santhosh, Green Level High School, under the supervision of Dr. Bharathi Pandi. This site describes research tools whose predictions have not yet been validated in a laboratory or a real pond, and it is not guidance for treating a waterbody.
