Selectivity Tool · How it works

Score the trade-off, keep the axes separate, then predict the pairs.

The tool combines two established methods (selectivity indices from toxicology and Bliss independence from combination pharmacology) into one framework for bloom control. Three steps, no black boxes.

1

Score

Selectivity Index = non-target EC50 divided by target EC50

SI = EC50 (bystander) ÷ EC50 (bloom)

EC50 is the dose that causes a 50% effect. It runs backwards from intuition: a low EC50 means harm arrives at a small dose, so a low number is a sensitive organism, not a safe one.

The ideal treatment kills the bloom at a low dose and only touches the bystanders at a much higher dose. That gap is the selectivity window, and SI measures it as a single number.

SI above 1: good

The bloom dies first. There is a dose that controls the bloom before the bystander is meaningfully harmed.

SI below 1: flagged

In the published tests, the bystander responded at a lower dose than the bloom did. The tool flags that axis.

2

Separate

Three non-target axes, tracked independently and never averaged

Green alga

Raphidocelis subcapitata

The primary producer. If it goes, the food web loses its base.

Water flea

Daphnia magna

The grazer. It eats algae and feeds fish, so it sits in the middle of everything.

Fish

Danio rerio

The vertebrate check. Standard proxy for vertebrate safety, including ours.

A treatment can score well for one group and poorly for another. Copper is the clearest case: it looks excellent against the green alga and much weaker against the water flea. Average those two numbers together and you get one comfortable-looking score that hides exactly the trade-off a lake manager needs to see.

So the axes are reported separately, and the headline number is min-SI, the worst axis of the three.

You are only as selective as your weakest link.

3

Combine

Predict what treatment pairs will do, before anyone runs the experiment

effect(A+B) = A + B − (A × B)

This is Bliss independence: the effect of A, plus the effect of B, minus the overlap you would otherwise count twice. It assumes the two agents act independently, which is a stated assumption rather than a claim.

Why it matters here: since the headline score is the weakest axis, the way to improve a treatment is to lift that axis. Pair something strong on the green alga axis with something strong on the water flea axis and the pair can outrank either one alone. The tool generates every single treatment and every pair, scores them on the same scale, and ranks them together.

The data gap

Most treatments have no target value yet.

For 13 treatments and 4 bloom-forming cyanobacteria, only 15 of the 52 treatment–genus pairs have a published value for how strongly the treatment hits the bloom itself. Harm to fish, water fleas and green algae is far better measured, because that is what regulatory testing is built for.

Framework complete. Laboratory validation planned.

Model predictions, not a treatment recommendation. CyanoSelect ranks treatments from published lab data and simulates ponds; none of its predictions has been tested in a real pond yet. Registered products have undergone risk assessment by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, and the product label governs how they may be used. Follow the label and your state’s rules, and consult a licensed aquatic applicator or your state agency before treating any water body.

Built by Arjun Pendharkar and Siddharth Santhosh, Green Level High School, under the supervision of Dr. Bharathi Pandi. This site describes research tools whose predictions have not yet been validated in a laboratory or a real pond, and it is not guidance for treating a waterbody.
