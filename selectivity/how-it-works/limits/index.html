Selectivity Tool · Limitations

What this tool does not tell you.

Read this before using the output. Every item here is a constraint we can state precisely, which is the point: each one is testable, and the planned lab work is designed to test them.

Predictions are not measurements.

Every combination value in the ranking is a hypothesis generated under stated assumptions. None of the combinations has been measured in a lab. Validation is planned, and both agreement and disagreement with the predictions are reportable results.

The input data is sparse.

The published literature contains comparable target-kill values for only a fraction of treatment and organism pairs. The rankings rest on relatively few fully scored treatments, so a single new measurement can move them.

Exposure durations do not always match.

Some published values compare target and non-target toxicity measured over different exposure times and endpoints. Because the target value sits in the denominator of the index, a mismatch inflates apparent selectivity. Affected entries are flagged in the code.

Bliss independence can be violated.

The combination model assumes the two agents act independently. Agents that share a mechanism (copper and hydrogen peroxide both act partly through oxidative stress) may not. Where the lab disagrees with the prediction, that disagreement is the finding.

It ranks by selectivity, not efficacy.

A treatment can kill the target extremely well and still rank poorly here. That is intended behavior, not a bug. This tool answers one question (what is the predicted risk to non-target organisms) and should sit alongside efficacy, cost, and permitting, not replace them.

Water chemistry changes toxicity.

Toxicity varies with water chemistry. Copper in particular is far more toxic in soft, low-alkalinity water than in hard water.

Strains differ.

Microcystis strains respond differently to the same product.

Microcystis is not the only bloom organism.

Other bloom organisms occur in North Carolina (for example Euglena and Prymnesium), and some treatments target green algae blooms instead.

Copper products are not all alike.

Chelated copper products are generally lower risk than copper sulfate. Copper is also toxic to mollusks, which this tool does not score.

Lab water is not lake water.

Lab values measured in clean water can differ substantially from values in natural water.

What's next

The predictions are the easy part. Testing them is the work.

The framework is complete. Everything below is what turns it from a set of predictions into validated findings.

1

Validation

Test the rankings in a lab

Planned

Three treatments that can be sourced as pure compounds and dosed precisely: copper sulfate, hydrogen peroxide, and diquat dibromide. Three organisms: Microcystis aeruginosa as the target, Raphidocelis subcapitata as the green alga, and Daphnia magna as the grazer.

3treatments
3organisms
~360test units

Each treatment and each predicted pair will be tested across a five-point dose series plus a control, following the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development test guidelines 201 for the algae and 202 for Daphnia. That comes to roughly 360 individual test units.

The rule that makes it worth doing: every organism is measured at the same exposure time using the same endpoint. Much of the published literature does not do this, and mismatched exposure times are one of the reasons reported selectivity can be inflated. Generating matched data is the point.

Question 01

Does the measured ranking match the predicted ranking?

Question 02

Does each combination beat, match, or fall short of its Bliss prediction? Beating it means real synergy. Matching it means the framework is calibrated. Falling short means antagonism.

All three outcomes are reportable. The experiment is designed so that it cannot be failed, only answered.

4

Scope

Extend what it can score

Longer term, three directions. Extend the ranking to the other three target genera once the data supports it. Add treatments beyond the current thirteen, including compounds that are promising but not commercially available. And test whether the Bliss assumption holds across mechanisms, since agents that share a mode of action are the most likely to break it.

The other two next steps: fill in the missing data and put it in front of practitioners.

Framework complete. Laboratory validation planned.

Model predictions, not a treatment recommendation. CyanoSelect ranks treatments from published lab data and simulates ponds; none of its predictions has been tested in a real pond yet. Registered products have undergone risk assessment by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, and the product label governs how they may be used. Follow the label and your state’s rules, and consult a licensed aquatic applicator or your state agency before treating any water body.

Built by Arjun Pendharkar and Siddharth Santhosh, Green Level High School, under the supervision of Dr. Bharathi Pandi. This site describes research tools whose predictions have not yet been validated in a laboratory or a real pond, and it is not guidance for treating a waterbody.
