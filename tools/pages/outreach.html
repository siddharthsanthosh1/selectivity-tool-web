Inside the Plant

Our visit to the Cary/Apex Water Treatment Facility

On September 30, 2026, we toured the Cary/Apex Water Treatment Facility, which treats Jordan Lake water for more than 310,000 people in Cary, Apex, Morrisville, and part of Research Triangle Park. The visit changed how we frame our project. Jordan Lake is operated by the US Army Corps of Engineers, and algae is not treated in the lake itself. Instead, the plant manages it on site, adjusting activated carbon to measured taste-and-odor compounds and using ozone and biofiltration. That is why our tool is aimed at ponds and smaller lakes, where in-lake treatment actually happens.

Our talk at the North Carolina Lake Management Society Fall Workshop

On October 15, 2026, we presented the Selectivity Tool at the North Carolina Lake Management Society Fall Workshop in Greensboro. The society brings together the people who look after the state's lakes and ponds. We opened with the June 2022 Jordan Lake case, walked through how the Selectivity Index compares treatments, and put our rankings in front of the people who make these decisions in the field.

Expert review and advice

Our biggest improvements came from people who told us what we had missed.

Expert review Dr. Rob Richardson

North Carolina State University. Works on aquatic weed management, and caught the mismatched diquat studies behind our September 2026 correction.

We had paired mismatched studies. Taking both numbers from the same study moved diquat from last to first among single treatments, and "match the tests" became the tool's central rule. Corrections.

Expert review Dr. JoAnn Burkholder

Center for Applied Aquatic Ecology, North Carolina State University. Her feedback on strain differences and sensitive species is shaping the next version.

What we learned from people in the field

The workshop let us learn directly from the professionals who manage North Carolina's lakes and ponds.

  • Advice from the North Carolina Department of Environmental Quality. We talked with Mark Vanderborgh of the North Carolina Department of Environmental Quality and took his advice on the project and on how a tool like ours fits the way blooms are handled in the state.
  • Conversations with practitioners. Between sessions we talked with lake managers, applicators and other people who work in the field about how treatment decisions actually get made, and what they would need from a tool like ours.
  • Presenting our solution. Explaining the tool to the North Carolina Lake Management Society, an audience that treats lakes and ponds for a living, pushed us to make the method clear and to be upfront about what published data can and cannot tell a lake manager.

What we heard is shaping the next version of the tool, alongside the laboratory validation we have planned.

What's next: outreach

Put it in front of practitioners

A tool that only makes sense to its authors is not useful. We have begun reaching out to watershed and riverkeeper organizations, state water quality agencies, and university researchers working on cyanobacterial blooms, to find out whether the framework reflects how treatment decisions actually get made in the field.

The most useful response so far has been expert review of the data and wording, which led to the September 2026 diquat correction and to several of the limitations we list.

One of four next steps. The others: lab validation, filling in the missing data and scope.
