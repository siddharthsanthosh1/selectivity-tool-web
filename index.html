Killing a toxic algae bloom shouldn't mean killing the lake.

Selectivity Tool is an open-source tool that ranks harmful algal bloom treatments by ecological selectivity: how well each one kills the bloom without killing everything else in the water.

The problem

A bloom is dangerous. So is the cure.

Toxic cyanobacteria blooms close beaches, threaten drinking water, and kill animals. The chemicals used to control them do not stop at the bloom.

Jordan Lake, North Carolina, June 2022

Dogs became ill after swimming at Jordan Lake, and at least one died. Chatham County issued a public warning about toxic algae in the water, and told people to keep themselves and their pets out of it.

ABC11: dog death linked to water toxins at Jordan Lake

The obvious response is to treat the water. Copper compounds, oxidants, and herbicides all knock a bloom down. The problem is what else they knock down.

These chemicals are largely non-selective. They hit the harmless green algae that feed the food web. They hit the water fleas that graze algae and become food for fish. Remove the green algae and the grazers starve. Remove the grazers and you have removed the animals that suppress algae naturally and feed everything above them.

Green algae

Harmless primary producers. The base of the food web, and the first thing an algaecide reaches.

Water fleas

Grazers that eat algae and feed fish. Lose them and the lake loses its natural brake on blooms.

Fish

The visible top of the system, and the reason most people care about the lake in the first place.

A treatment that clears a bloom but sterilizes the food web is not a solution. It is a trade, and right now that trade is usually made without measuring it.

Most research asks what kills the bloom. Almost none asks what kills the bloom while sparing everything else.

How it works

Score the trade-off, keep the axes separate, then predict the pairs.

The tool combines two established methods (selectivity indices from toxicology and Bliss independence from combination pharmacology) into one framework for bloom control. Three steps, no black boxes.

1

Score

Selectivity Index = non-target EC50 divided by target EC50

SI = EC50 (bystander) ÷ EC50 (bloom)

EC50 is the dose that causes a 50% effect. It runs backwards from intuition: a low EC50 means harm arrives at a small dose, so a low number is a sensitive organism, not a safe one.

The ideal treatment kills the bloom at a low dose and only touches the bystanders at a much higher dose. That gap is the selectivity window, and SI measures it as a single number.

SI above 1: good

The bloom dies first. There is a dose that controls the bloom before the bystander is meaningfully harmed.

SI below 1: disqualifying

The bystander is harmed at a lower dose than the bloom needs. There is no safe operating dose, so the treatment fails on that axis.

2

Separate

Three non-target axes, tracked independently and never averaged

Green alga

Raphidocelis subcapitata

The primary producer. If it goes, the food web loses its base.

Water flea

Daphnia magna

The grazer. It eats algae and feeds fish, so it sits in the middle of everything.

Fish

Danio rerio

The vertebrate check. Standard proxy for vertebrate safety, including ours.

A treatment can spare one group and devastate another. Copper is the clearest case: it looks excellent against the green alga and much weaker against the water flea. Average those two numbers together and you get one comfortable-looking score that hides exactly the trade-off a lake manager needs to see.

So the axes are reported separately, and the headline number is min-SI, the worst axis of the three.

You are only as selective as your weakest link.

3

Combine

Predict what treatment pairs will do, before anyone runs the experiment

effect(A+B) = A + B − (A × B)

This is Bliss independence: the effect of A, plus the effect of B, minus the overlap you would otherwise count twice. It assumes the two agents act independently, which is a stated assumption rather than a claim.

Why it matters here: since the headline score is the weakest axis, the way to improve a treatment is to lift that axis. Pair something strong on the green alga axis with something strong on the water flea axis and the pair can outrank either one alone. The tool generates every single treatment and every pair, scores them on the same scale, and ranks them together.

Three findings

What shows up once you actually score the trade-off.

None of these are visible in a kill-only evaluation, which is how most treatments are compared today.

Finding 01

Copper's selectivity collapses exactly when you'd use it.

Published data shows copper's effective dose against a bloom rises roughly tenfold as bloom cell density climbs. Denser bloom, more copper needed. At realistic bloom densities its water flea selectivity index drops below 1, which means it harms the water flea before it controls the bloom. Copper ranks first in the table above, and it is still the treatment most sensitive to the conditions you would actually deploy it in.

10xrise in effective dose

Finding 02

Effective does not mean selective.

Diquat kills Microcystis well. It also harms the harmless green alga at an even lower dose, giving a selectivity index of 0.43. There is no dose that clears the bloom without taking the green alga first. A kill-only evaluation would look at the same data and record a success.

SI 0.43green alga axis, disqualifying

Finding 03

The data to answer this question barely exists.

Compiling published toxicity data across 13 treatments and 4 cyanobacteria genera, only 13 of 52 target-kill cells could be filled. The non-target side of the ledger is comparatively rich, because harm to fish, water fleas, and green algae is what regulatory testing is built to measure. How well a chemical kills the bloom itself is measured far less consistently.

Limitations

What this tool does not tell you.

Read this before using the output. Every item here is a constraint we can state precisely, which is the point: each one is testable, and the lab work in progress is designed to test them.

Predictions are not measurements.

Every combination value in the ranking is a hypothesis generated under stated assumptions. None of the combinations has been measured in a lab. Validation is in progress, and both agreement and disagreement with the predictions are reportable results.

The input data is sparse.

The published literature contains comparable target-kill values for only a fraction of treatment and organism pairs. The rankings rest on relatively few fully scored treatments, so a single new measurement can move them.

Exposure durations do not always match.

Some published values compare target and non-target toxicity measured over different exposure times and endpoints. Because the target value sits in the denominator of the index, a mismatch inflates apparent selectivity. Affected entries are flagged in the code.

Bliss independence can be violated.

The combination model assumes the two agents act independently. Agents that share a mechanism (copper and hydrogen peroxide both act partly through oxidative stress) may not. Where the lab disagrees with the prediction, that disagreement is the finding.

It ranks by selectivity, not efficacy.

A treatment can kill the target extremely well and still rank poorly here. That is intended behavior, not a bug. This tool answers one question (what is the collateral cost) and should sit alongside efficacy, cost, and permitting, not replace them.

Who it is for

Anyone who has to choose a treatment and then live with the lake afterward.

The output is a ranked table with the reasoning attached, so it can be read, argued with, and reproduced.

Lake managers

Comparing treatment options for a specific waterbody.

Water utilities

Protecting source water without collateral damage downstream.

Watershed nonprofits

Asking informed questions about a proposed treatment plan.

Researchers

Generating testable hypotheses about treatment combinations.

Regulators

Weighing permit decisions against non-target impact.

What's next

The predictions are the easy part. Testing them is the work.

The framework is complete. Everything below is what turns it from a set of predictions into validated findings.

1

Validation

Test the rankings in a lab

In progress

Three treatments that can be sourced as pure compounds and dosed precisely: copper sulfate, hydrogen peroxide, and diquat dibromide. Three organisms: Microcystis aeruginosa as the target, Raphidocelis subcapitata as the green alga, and Daphnia magna as the grazer.

3treatments
3organisms
~360test units

Each treatment and each predicted pair is tested across a five-point dose series plus a control, following OECD 201 for the algae and OECD 202 for Daphnia. That comes to roughly 360 individual test units.

The rule that makes it worth doing: every organism is measured at the same exposure time using the same endpoint. Much of the published literature does not do this, and mismatched exposure times are one of the reasons reported selectivity can be inflated. Generating matched data is the point.

Question 01

Does the measured ranking match the predicted ranking?

Question 02

Does each combination beat, match, or fall short of its Bliss prediction? Beating it means real synergy. Matching it means the framework is calibrated. Falling short means antagonism.

All three outcomes are reportable. The experiment is designed so that it cannot be failed, only answered.

2

Data

Fill in the missing data

Only 13 of 52 target-kill cells are currently filled. Some of those gaps are genuine absences in the literature, and some are values locked behind paywalls or buried in regulatory documents. Every cell filled makes the ranking more stable. Priorities are additional target-kill values for the three under-covered cyanobacteria genera, and cross-validating the values that currently rest on a single source.

3

Outreach

Put it in front of practitioners

A tool that only makes sense to its authors is not useful. We have begun reaching out to watershed and riverkeeper organizations, state water quality agencies, and university researchers working on cyanobacterial blooms, to find out whether the framework reflects how treatment decisions actually get made in the field.

The most useful response so far has been confirmation that the underlying gap is real: treatment is often evaluated on removing harmful algae without accounting for the effect on the rest of the aquatic system.

4

Scope

Extend what it can score

Longer term, three directions. Extend the ranking to the other three target genera once the data supports it. Add treatments beyond the current thirteen, including compounds that are promising but not commercially available. And test whether the Bliss assumption holds across mechanisms, since agents that share a mode of action are the most likely to break it.
